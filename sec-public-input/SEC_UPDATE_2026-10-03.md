# 1. Executive update for readers

The SEC's modernization of securities infrastructure has now moved beyond transfer-agent recordkeeping into crypto custody.

On Sept. 1, 2026, the SEC proposed a comprehensive modernization of its transfer-agent rules in **File No. S7-2026-30**, including changes intended to reflect electronic recordkeeping, electronic communications and the use of blockchain technology in securities offerings and transfers.

TitleChain Foundation submitted its original public comment on **Sept. 5, 2026**. That submission is publicly listed on the SEC docket.

On **Oct. 1, 2026**, the SEC opened a second proceeding, **File No. S7-2026-35 — Adviser and Regulated Fund Custody Rules; Crypto Custody Rules**. The proposal creates a tailored framework for investment advisers and regulated funds to custody covered crypto assets, addresses state trust companies, establishes a limited adviser self-custody pathway, and raises questions involving transfer agents, uncertificated digital securities and software-based transaction authority.

On **Oct. 3, 2026**, TitleChain Foundation submitted two additional filings:

1. A **Supplemental Comment to S7-2026-30**, addressing custody, cryptographic control, transfer authority and the authoritative securityholder record.
2. A **standalone Comment on S7-2026-35**, addressing custody, key control, state trust companies, adviser self-custody, transfer-agent custody of digital securities, and software / AI agents acting under delegated authority.

**Current status:** Both Oct. 3 submissions have been sent to the SEC and are awaiting appearance on the SEC public-comment pages.

# 2. Updated Foundation assessment

The two SEC proposals should now be assessed together.

The transfer-agent proposal asks how ownership records, transfer processing and market infrastructure should operate in an electronic and blockchain-enabled environment.

The custody proposal asks who may safeguard crypto assets and key material, under what controls, and under what circumstances an adviser may directly hold key material for a client.

Taken together, the proposals expose a central issue:

> **Custody, technical control, legal authority and legal ownership are related, but they are not the same thing.**

TitleChain Foundation's updated position is:

> **A private key establishes an ability to act; it does not, standing alone, establish the legal right to act.**

And:

> **Custody should protect the asset. It should not become the source of legal authority over the asset.**

These principles should anchor the SEC section going forward.

# 3. Why the custody proposal matters to transfer agents

The Oct. 1 custody proposal strengthens the relevance of the Foundation's Sept. 5 transfer-agent comment.

A digital security can involve several distinct records and actors:

- the investor or beneficial owner;
- the securityholder recognized on the issuer's official books;
- the transfer agent maintaining the authoritative securityholder record;
- a custodian safeguarding the asset or key material;
- an investment adviser exercising authority for a client;
- a wallet or account associated with the asset;
- a cryptographic key capable of moving the asset;
- a broker-dealer or other intermediary;
- software, smart contracts or AI agents capable of initiating or executing instructions.

The regulatory problem is not solved merely by knowing which wallet has a key.

A reliable system must be able to establish:

- whose asset it is;
- who is authorized to act;
- in what legal or fiduciary capacity;
- what restrictions apply;
- what instruction was authorized;
- who or what executed it;
- what transaction occurred;
- and what legally recognized ownership state resulted.

The Foundation's position is that these functions should be **interoperable without being collapsed into one function or one vendor**.

- SEC Crypto Newsroom: https://www.sec.gov/about/crypto-task-force/crypto-newsroom

## What the SEC proposal changes

The SEC says the proposal is intended to provide a tailored framework for custody of crypto assets by registered investment advisers and regulated funds and to remove regulatory barriers that have limited advisers' ability to provide crypto-related investment advice.

Key areas relevant to TitleChain Foundation include:

### A. Permitted crypto custody

The proposal creates a clearer pathway for covered crypto assets to be held through permitted custodial arrangements instead of requiring exposure to exist only through wrappers such as exchange-traded products.

**Do not state:** "The SEC now lets every adviser buy every token."

**Use instead:**  
"The proposal creates a regulatory custody pathway for covered crypto assets, subject to the asset, account, adviser or fund, and the applicable federal securities laws."

### B. State trust companies

The SEC proposal contemplates state trust companies as custody providers subject to the proposal's requirements.

**Assessment:** This may materially expand the competitive custody layer, but the site must not imply that every state trust company automatically qualifies for every asset or client.

### C. Limited adviser self-custody

"Self-custody" in this SEC proposal does **not** mean ordinary investor self-custody.

It refers to circumstances in which the **investment adviser itself holds or controls key material associated with the client's crypto asset** rather than using an outside permitted custodian.

The pathway is limited and conditioned. The adviser generally must determine that an outside permitted custodian is unavailable for the asset and periodically reassess that conclusion, while complying with safeguarding, key-management, cybersecurity, segregation, recordkeeping and authorization controls.

**Reader-facing explanation:**

> In this proposal, "adviser self-custody" is a regulated fallback in which the adviser safeguards client crypto directly. It is not a general declaration that a private wallet replaces regulated custody.

### D. Transfer agents and uncertificated digital securities

The proposal raises questions involving transfer agents and custody of **uncertificated digital securities**.

"Uncertificated" is a standard securities-law term meaning that the security is not represented by a physical paper certificate; ownership is reflected in the issuer's or transfer agent's official records.

**Foundation assessment:** A transfer agent may potentially perform a custody role without eliminating the separate responsibility to maintain or reconcile the authoritative ownership record.

### E. Software / AI transaction authority

As financial software becomes capable of initiating and executing transactions, the Foundation's position is that technical ability must not be confused with legal authority.

Use:

> **A machine may execute an authorized instruction. It should not create the authority on which the instruction depends.**

Authority should originate with an accountable human or legal entity and should be scoped, time-bound, auditable, revocable and linked to the relevant role or capacity.

# 9. Recommended "What changed?" callout

## What changed after our first SEC filing?

When TitleChain Foundation filed its original transfer-agent comment on Sept. 5, the Commission was considering how a decades-old transfer-agent framework should operate in an electronic and blockchain-enabled market.

On Oct. 1, the SEC opened a second proceeding addressing the custody of crypto assets.

That matters because custody introduces a second record of control into the transaction.

The custodian may hold the key.  
The adviser may have authority to act for the client.  
The transfer agent may maintain the securityholder record.  
The blockchain may record the transaction.  
A software agent may execute it.

Those facts can all be true at the same time.

The regulatory system therefore needs a way to prove not merely that a transaction occurred, but **who had the authority to cause it and what legally recognized ownership state resulted.**

That is the issue TitleChain Foundation's Oct. 3 submissions place into both SEC proceedings.

# 10. Recommended "Why this matters" section

## Why this matters

Digitizing a security does not eliminate the legal relationships behind it.

As securities and other assets move into programmable infrastructure, the industry will need to distinguish:

**Control** — who can technically move the asset.

**Custody** — who safeguards the asset or key material.

**Authority** — who has the legal right to direct an action.

**Ownership** — who holds the legally recognized interest.

**Registration** — who is reflected on the official securityholder record.

**Execution** — what system carries out the instruction.

**Evidence** — what proves why the transaction was permitted and what resulted.

TitleChain Foundation's position is that modernization should make those relationships more verifiable, not erase them.
