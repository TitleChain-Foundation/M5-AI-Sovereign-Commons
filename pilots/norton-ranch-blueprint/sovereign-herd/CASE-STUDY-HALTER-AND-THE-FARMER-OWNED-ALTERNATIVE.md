# Case Study — One Model of Connected Agriculture, and the Choice We Want to Add

**Status:** Public research note
**Reviewed:** October 3, 2026

Halter is a useful current example because it shows both what modern agricultural technology can do and why an open, farmer-controlled alternative matters.

This note is not an accusation of wrongdoing and is not a claim that Halter's technology lacks value. It compares business and data architectures.

## Verified current facts

On March 25, 2026, Halter announced a **$220 million Series E** at a **$2 billion valuation**, led by **Founders Fund**, with participation from additional investors. Halter said at the time that it served more than 2,000 ranchers and farmers across New Zealand, Australia and the United States and had sold one million solar-powered collars.

Halter's own technology page says each collar collects and sends more than **6,000 data points per minute** to Halter's cloud-based platform and that the company has collected more than **7 billion hours of animal-behavior data**. Halter calls its virtual-fencing behavior system the **Cowgorithm®**.

Halter's April 2026 U.S. Terms of Service describe subscription fees, an initial term, automatic renewal and updated pricing at renewal.

Halter's public site does not present one universal per-animal U.S. price. Independent 2026 reporting has described subscription pricing in approximately the **$5–$8 per animal per month** range, with pricing varying by market, herd size and implementation. That figure should therefore be presented as a reported range, not a universal Halter tariff.

## The architectural choice

Halter's model demonstrates one path:

```text
COLLAR
   ↓
VENDOR PLATFORM / CLOUD
   ↓
VENDOR MODELS + SERVICES
   ↓
SUBSCRIPTION
   ↓
FARMER APPLICATION
```

The Sovereign Herd reference model demonstrates another:

```text
COLLAR / SENSOR
   ↓
FARM-CONTROLLED GATEWAY
   ↓
LOCAL DATA STORE
   ↓
LOCAL / FARM-SELECTED MODELS
   ↓
FARMER-CONTROLLED HISTORY
```

Remote services can still be added when the farmer chooses them.

## The principle

The point is not that recurring subscriptions are inherently wrong. The point is that they should not be the only practical route to modern agricultural intelligence.

A farmer should have the option to buy or finance hardware, run compatible software locally, preserve the herd's history independently of the device vendor, and hire competitive service providers without losing the underlying data.

## Why an open commons matters

A venture-backed company can spend hundreds of millions of dollars building a proprietary system. A small agriculture company cannot.

The Commons changes the starting line.

If public research, permissively licensed models, standard schemas, reference firmware patterns, device provisioning, local storage, provenance and conformance tools are already available, then an independent entrepreneur can compete on the thing they actually know: cattle, dairy, bees, orchards, flowers, soil, grazing, water, equipment, local installation, veterinary or agronomic workflows, and regional service.

That is how a public technical commons can become local economic infrastructure.
